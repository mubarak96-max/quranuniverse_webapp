Al-Masad, meaning drawn

Each picture shows a relationship, not just an object: what leaves and what returns, what is offered and what is refused, what is carried and what is worn. Two or three quiet labels, one dotted connector, same light and grain as before.

111:1AL-MASAD

His uncle cursed him. Allah returned the curse, forever.

The Prophet ﷺ climbed a hill, called all of Makkah, and warned them. His own uncle stood up, dusted off his hands: "Is this why you gathered us? May you perish." The one man whose job was to protect him.

The relative who mocks your choices at every gathering. The one who should have your back and laughs the loudest instead. You keep hoping blood will fix it.

Family will come around if I keep proving myself.

Some will not. Allah defended His Prophet when his uncle would not. Your worth does not wait on their approval.

Stop rehearsing the argument with that relative. Do your work. Let the record be written elsewhere.

111:2AL-MASAD

Money and sons bought him nothing.

Abu Lahab was rich, connected, with grown sons. Warned about the Day, he said: if it is true, I will ransom myself with my wealth and my children. This verse is the reply.

The savings account. The pension. The kids who will "take care of me." The network. Everything you quietly count on when the thought of death passes through.

What I own and who I raised are my safety net.

They are blessings for here, not currency for there. Nothing I own crosses with me except what I did with it.

Name one thing you are secretly relying on instead of Allah. Say it out loud.

111:3AL-MASAD

Father of Flame. Allah told him where the flame ends.

His real name was Abdul-Uzza. People called him Abu Lahab because his face shone, and he loved the name. The verse turns it over: the flame will not be on his face. He will be inside it.

The reputation you polish. The title, the nickname, the way people describe you at parties. Built on what? If it is built on someone else's back, it has an ending.

A good name is what people call me.

A good name is what my deeds will be called on that Day. The rest is lighting.

Ask what one person you have wronged would call you. Fix that before polishing anything else.

111:4AL-MASAD

She carried the wood that fed his fire.

Umm Jamil, sister of Abu Sufyan, one of the leading women of Quraysh. She scattered thorns on the path the Prophet ﷺ walked at night. She was not dragged into her husband's hatred. She fed it.

The friend who, every time you are angry, adds a story. The spouse who never says "let it go." The group chat that keeps a grudge alive for years. Wood carriers.

I am only reacting to what others started.

Feeding a fire is its own deed. Carrying the wood counts the same as lighting it.

Think of one grudge you keep warm for someone else. Put the wood down.

111:5AL-MASAD

The rope she carried harm with, now on her neck.

A rope of palm fiber was a worker's tool: bind the bundle, carry it on your back. She used hers to carry harm. The surah ends with the same rope, twisted, on her neck. The tool became the sentence.

The sharp tongue that wins every argument. The talent for finding people's weak spots. The screenshot folder. Whatever you use to hurt, you are also tying to yourself.

My weapons are separate from me. I pick them up, I put them down.

What I carry for others, I wear. The rope goes both ways.

Delete one thing you kept as a weapon.

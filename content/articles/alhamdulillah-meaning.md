Alhamdulillah (*al-hamdu lillah*, الحمد لله) is an Arabic phrase meaning "all praise is for God" or "praise be to God." It is the first verse of the Quran after the opening "In the name of God" (Quran 1:2). Muslims say it after good news, after eating, after sneezing and even in hardship, to acknowledge that every blessing comes from God.

If you ask a Muslim friend "How are you?", the answer will often be "Alhamdulillah." It works like "thank God" in English, but it carries more weight: it is a statement of belief that God deserves praise in every situation. This guide covers the meaning, the Arabic, the Quranic origin, the sneezing etiquette, how to reply, and what psychology says about the habit of gratitude.

## Key takeaways

- Alhamdulillah means "all praise is for God," from *al* (the, all), *hamd* (praise), *li* (for, belonging to) and *Allah* (God).
- It is the opening line of Surah al-Fatihah after the basmala, "[All] praise is [due] to Allah, Lord of the worlds" (Quran 1:2), recited in every unit of every prayer.
- The Prophet taught that a person who sneezes should say "alhamdulillah" and the listener should reply "yarhamuk Allah" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6224).
- The Prophet said that "alhamdulillah fills the scale" of good deeds (Sahih Muslim 223).
- The Quran promises, "If you are grateful, I will surely increase you" (Quran 14:7).

## What does alhamdulillah mean?

The phrase is built from four parts:

1. **Al**: "the," which in Arabic can carry the sense of "all" or "every."
2. **Hamd**: "praise," specifically praise of someone for their perfect qualities and their kindness.
3. **Li**: "for" or "belonging to."
4. **Allah**: the Arabic word for God.

So *al-hamdu lillah* means that all true praise, every kind of it, belongs to God. When a person praises a beautiful sunset, a skilled doctor or a good meal, the deepest source of that beauty, skill and nourishment is God. Alhamdulillah sends the praise back to its origin.

**Hamd** (*hamd*, حمد) is one of the key words of Islamic devotion. One of God's names, *al-Hamid*, comes from the same root and means "the Praiseworthy." You can read about it on the [al-Hamid name page](/names/al-hamid).

## Alhamdulillah in Arabic and how to spell it

In Arabic, alhamdulillah is written الحمد لله, two words: *al-hamdu* (the praise) and *lillah* (for God). In English you will see several spellings.

| Spelling | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Alhamdulillah | The most common English spelling |
| Alhamdulilah | A frequent variant with one "l" |
| Al-hamdu lillah | Separates the two Arabic words; common in academic writing |
| Al hamdu lillah | Same as above without the hyphen |
| Elhamdulillah | Common in Turkish and Bosnian usage |

All of these refer to the same phrase. The double "l" in *lillah* reflects the doubled letter *lam* in the Arabic.

## Where does alhamdulillah come from? Quran 1:2

Alhamdulillah is the first thing the Quran says after its opening line. Surah al-Fatihah, the chapter Muslims recite in every unit of prayer, begins with the basmala and then:

> [All] praise is [due] to Allah, Lord of the worlds -
>
> — [Quran 1:2](/surah/al-fatihah/2)

Because of this, every Muslim who prays the five daily prayers says alhamdulillah at least seventeen times a day in the obligatory units alone. Sahih Muslim 395 records a sacred hadith in which God says that when His servant recites "Praise be to Allah, the Lord of the worlds," He answers, "My servant has praised Me." Our guide to [Surah al-Fatihah's meaning](/learn/surah-al-fatihah-meaning) explains the rest of the chapter verse by verse.

The phrase also frames the Quran's picture of the afterlife. The people of Paradise end their prayers with it: "And the last of their call will be, 'Praise to Allah, Lord of the worlds!'" ([10:10](/surah/yunus/10)). When they enter Paradise they say, "Praise to Allah, who has guided us to this" ([7:43](/surah/al-araf/43)). Alhamdulillah, in other words, is both the first word of the Quran's opening prayer and the last word of the believers' story.

## Hamd vs shukr: praise and thanks

Arabic has two words that English often merges into "thanks": *hamd* and *shukr*. Classical commentators, including Ibn Kathir in his explanation of 1:2, distinguish them.

| Feature | Hamd (praise) | Shukr (gratitude) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arabic | حمد | شكر |
| What prompts it | Who God is, and what He gives | A favor that has been received |
| How it is expressed | Mainly with the tongue and heart | With the heart, tongue and actions |
| Can it be given with no favor received? | Yes, God is praised for His perfection | No, it responds to a gift |
| Example phrase | Alhamdulillah | Ash-shukru lillah, or using a blessing well |

Put simply, *hamd* is wider in its reasons, and *shukr* is wider in its forms. You praise God even when nothing new has happened, because He is worthy of praise. You thank Him by saying so and by using His gifts in ways that please Him. The Quran tells Luqman, "Be grateful to Allah. And whoever is grateful is grateful for [the benefit of] himself" ([31:12](/surah/luqman/12)). God's name *ash-Shakur*, "the Appreciative," is covered on the [ash-Shakur name page](/names/ash-shakur).

## Why do Muslims say alhamdulillah after sneezing?

This is one of the most searched questions about the phrase, and the answer comes straight from the hadith. The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) said:

> "If anyone of you sneezes, he should say 'Al-Hamdulillah' (Praise be to Allah), and his (Muslim) brother or companion should say to him, 'Yarhamuk Allah' (May Allah bestow His mercy on you). When the latter says 'Yarhamuk Allah', the former should say, 'Yahdikumullah wa yuslih balakum' (May Allah give you guidance and improve your condition)."
>
> — Sahih al-Bukhari 6224 (transliteration standardized)

The exchange has three steps:

| Who | Says | Arabic | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The person who sneezes | Alhamdulillah | الحمد لله | All praise is for God |
| The person who hears | Yarhamuk Allah | يرحمك الله | May God have mercy on you |
| The person who sneezed | Yahdikumullah wa yuslih balakum | يهديكم الله ويصلح بالكم | May God guide you and set your affairs right |

The reason behind it appears in a neighboring hadith: "Allah likes sneezing and dislikes yawning" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6223). A sneeze clears the body and brings relief, so it is treated as a small blessing worth praising God for. The same hadith describes the listener's reply as a duty on Muslims who hear the sneezer praise God. It is a short daily exchange of prayers between two people.

## When do Muslims say alhamdulillah?

Beyond sneezing, the Prophet's practice ties the phrase to many moments of the day:

- **On waking.** He would say, "Praise is to Allah who gave us life after He caused us to die, and to Him is the return" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6312).
- **After eating and drinking.** "Allah is pleased with His servant who says al-hamdu lillah" over food and drink (Sahih Muslim 2734).
- **After prayer.** Muslims say alhamdulillah 33 times, alongside subhanallah and Allahu akbar (Sahih Muslim 597).
- **On hearing good news.** "We got the house, alhamdulillah."
- **In answer to "How are you?"** Whether life is going well or not.

The Prophet also called it the best form of supplication: "The best remembrance is *la ilaha illallah*, and the best supplication is *al-hamdulillah*" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3383, graded hasan). Praise is itself a way of asking, because it shows the heart turned toward the Giver. Many of these moments appear in our guide to [daily duas](/learn/daily-duas).

## Alhamdulillah in every situation

Muslims often say *alhamdulillah 'ala kulli hal*, "praise be to God in every circumstance." Sunan Ibn Majah 3803 reports that when the Prophet saw something he liked, he praised God "by whose grace good deeds are completed," and when he saw something he disliked, he said, "Praise is to Allah in all circumstances." Al-Albani graded this narration hasan, while some other hadith scholars considered its chain weak.

The idea itself is firmly rooted in authentic sources. The Prophet said: "Strange are the ways of a believer, for there is good in every affair of his... if he has an occasion to feel delight, he thanks (God)... and if he gets into trouble and shows resignation (and endures it patiently), there is a good for him in it" (Sahih Muslim 2999).

This does not mean pretending pain is pleasant. It means trusting that God's wisdom is at work even when you cannot see it, a theme explored in our guide to [sabr, patience in Islam](/learn/sabr-patience-in-islam).

## What does research say about gratitude?

The habit of praising God is a spiritual act first, but psychology offers an interesting parallel. In a widely cited 2003 study in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, psychologists Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked participants to keep regular lists of things they were grateful for, while other groups listed hassles or neutral events ([Emmons and McCullough, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.2.377)).

Across three studies, including one with adults living with neuromuscular disease, the gratitude groups showed higher well-being on several, though not all, of the measures. The most consistent effect was on positive mood.

These studies looked at general gratitude, not religious praise, so they cannot measure what alhamdulillah means to a believer. They do suggest that a regular habit of noticing blessings, which the phrase builds into every day, can shape how people feel.

## How do you reply to alhamdulillah?

The reply depends on the situation:

1. **After a sneeze**, reply "yarhamuk Allah," as in the hadith above.
2. **When someone answers "How are you?" with alhamdulillah**, you can say "alhamdulillah" back, or ask a follow-up question as you would in any conversation.
3. **When someone shares good news with alhamdulillah**, you can say "mashallah" or "barakallahu fik" (may God bless you). Our guide to [mashallah meaning](/learn/mashallah-meaning) explains both.

Non-Muslims can respond naturally in English. "That's great to hear" or "bless you" after a sneeze is entirely polite.

## Common mistakes with alhamdulillah

- **Treating it only as "thank you."** It is broader: praise for who God is, not just for a favor.
- **Saying it only when things go well.** The Prophet's example shows praise in hardship too.
- **Using it for the future.** Plans need inshallah, not alhamdulillah. See [inshallah meaning](/learn/inshallah-meaning).
- **Saying the words while complaining.** The phrase is meant to settle the heart, not decorate a grievance.

## Alhamdulillah and the other phrases of remembrance

Alhamdulillah is one of four short phrases that run through Muslim devotion, often recited together after prayer or counted on prayer beads.

| Phrase | Arabic | Meaning | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [SubhanAllah](/learn/subhanallah-meaning) | سبحان الله | Glory be to God | God is free of every flaw |
| Alhamdulillah | الحمد لله | All praise is for God | God deserves all praise |
| Allahu akbar | الله أكبر | God is greater | God is greater than all things |
| La ilaha illallah | لا إله إلا الله | There is no god but God | God alone is worshipped |

Sahih Muslim 223 records that "subhanallah and alhamdulillah fill up what is between the heavens and the earth." These phrases are the core of **dhikr**, the remembrance of God, explained in our guide to [what dhikr is](/learn/what-is-dhikr).

## Frequently asked questions

### What does alhamdulillah mean?

Alhamdulillah means "all praise is for God" or "praise be to God." It is formed from *al* (the), *hamd* (praise), *li* (for) and *Allah* (God). It is the second verse of Surah al-Fatihah (Quran 1:2), and Muslims say it in prayer, after eating, after sneezing and whenever they want to acknowledge God's blessings.

### How do you write alhamdulillah in Arabic?

Alhamdulillah is written الحمد لله in Arabic. It is two words: *al-hamdu*, "the praise," and *lillah*, "for God." Common English spellings include alhamdulillah, alhamdulilah and al-hamdu lillah. All of them refer to the same phrase found in the opening chapter of the Quran.

### Why do Muslims say alhamdulillah after sneezing?

The Prophet taught it. Sahih al-Bukhari 6224 records that a person who sneezes should say "alhamdulillah," the listener replies "yarhamuk Allah" (may God have mercy on you), and the sneezer answers "yahdikumullah wa yuslih balakum." Another hadith (Sahih al-Bukhari 6223) says God likes sneezing, so it is treated as a blessing.

### How do you reply to alhamdulillah?

After a sneeze, reply "yarhamuk Allah," meaning "may God have mercy on you." In everyday conversation, you can say "alhamdulillah" back, or respond to good news with "mashallah" or "barakallahu fik." There is no single required reply outside the sneezing etiquette, and plain English responses are fine.

### Is alhamdulillah the same as "thank God"?

They are close, and "thank God" is a reasonable everyday translation. Alhamdulillah is broader, though. *Hamd* means praising God for His perfect qualities as well as for His gifts, while thanks usually responds to a specific favor. That is why Muslims say alhamdulillah even when nothing particular has happened.

### What does alhamdulillah ala kulli hal mean?

*Alhamdulillah 'ala kulli hal* means "praise be to God in every circumstance." Sunan Ibn Majah 3803 reports that the Prophet said it when he saw something he disliked; al-Albani graded the narration hasan, though some scholars considered it weak. The idea matches Sahih Muslim 2999, which says every affair of a believer is good.

Islam holds that God's existence can be known by reason as well as by revelation. The Quran itself argues that the universe did not come from nothing and did not create itself, so it needs a Creator. Muslim philosophers such as al-Ghazali and Ibn Sina built formal arguments from this idea, and versions of them are still debated in philosophy departments today.

None of these arguments works like a laboratory test, and every one has serious critics. This guide sets out the six lines of reasoning most discussed by philosophers and theologians, where each came from, and the strongest objections to it, so you can weigh them yourself. It is written for sceptics and students as much as for believers.

## Key takeaways

- The Quran presents a short argument from causation: "Or were they created by nothing, or were they the creators [of themselves]?" ([Quran 52:35](/surah/at-tur/35)).
- The kalam cosmological argument is named after Islamic theology (*kalam*); al-Ghazali stated it in *al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad*, and William Lane Craig revived it in *The Kalam Cosmological Argument* (1979).
- Ibn Sina (Avicenna) argued that contingent things depend on a "Necessary Existent," a proof later known as the Proof of the Truthful (*burhan al-siddiqin*).
- Physicists Geraint Lewis and Luke Barnes document the fine-tuning of physical constants in *A Fortunate Universe* (Cambridge University Press, 2016), while disagreeing on whether God or a multiverse explains it.
- The Quran calls belief in God part of human nature (*fitrah*, [Quran 30:30](/surah/ar-rum/30)); psychologist Justin Barrett's *Born Believers* (2012) argues that children's minds readily accept the idea of a god.

## Proof or evidence? Framing the question

In mathematics, a proof forces agreement. In philosophy, an argument is judged by whether its premises are more plausible than their denials. Most philosophers of religion, believers and atheists alike, treat arguments for God as a cumulative case: each adds weight without settling the matter alone.

Islamic tradition has long held something similar. The Quran calls the created world *ayat*, "signs," and repeatedly invites reflection rather than demanding blind acceptance:

> We will show them Our signs in the horizons and within themselves until it becomes clear to them that it is the truth. But is it not sufficient concerning your Lord that He is, over all things, a Witness?
>
> — [Quran 41:53](/surah/fussilat/53)

## The Quran's own argument: created by nothing?

Long before formal philosophy, the Quran posed a question in [Surah at-Tur](/surah/at-tur):

> Or were they created by nothing, or were they the creators [of themselves]?
>
> — [Quran 52:35](/surah/at-tur/35)

> Or did they create the heavens and the earth? Rather, they are not certain.
>
> — [Quran 52:36](/surah/at-tur/36)

The verses list the options: human beings came from nothing, made themselves, or made the universe. Each is absurd, which leaves a Creator who is not part of the chain. Muslim theologians developed this into the formal arguments below. The divine name [al-Khaliq](/names/al-khaliq), "the Creator," sums up the conclusion.

## The kalam cosmological argument (al-Ghazali to Craig)

The best-known modern argument for God carries an Arabic name. *Kalam* is the Islamic discipline of rational theology, and its theologians argued that the universe had a beginning and therefore a cause.

Al-Ghazali (d. 1111) gave a compact version in *al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad* ("Moderation in Belief"): every being that begins has a cause for its beginning; the world is a being that begins; therefore it has a cause. He defended the second premise by arguing that an actually infinite series of past events is impossible.

In 1979 the philosopher William Lane Craig published *The Kalam Cosmological Argument* in the Library of Philosophy and Religion series, explicitly tracing the argument to al-Ghazali. Craig's version runs:

1. Everything that begins to exist has a cause.
2. The universe began to exist.
3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.

Craig supports premise 2 with philosophical arguments against an actual infinite and with Big Bang cosmology. He then argues that a cause of all space, time and matter must be timeless, immaterial and enormously powerful.

**Main objections.** Critics question whether causation applies to the universe as a whole, since our idea of cause comes from events inside time. Some cosmologists propose models without an absolute beginning, and the Big Bang marks the limit of current physics rather than settled proof of a first moment. Others cite quantum events that seem uncaused, though defenders reply that a quantum vacuum is still something, not nothing. It is worth knowing that Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd, both Muslims, held that the world could be eternal, so this argument was disputed inside Islamic philosophy too.

## The contingency argument (Ibn Sina's Proof of the Truthful)

Ibn Sina (Avicenna, d. 1037) took a different route that does not depend on whether the universe had a beginning. He distinguished between things that are **contingent** (they could fail to exist and depend on something else) and a being that is **necessary** (*wajib al-wujud*), which exists by its own nature.

His reasoning, found in works such as *al-Isharat wa al-Tanbihat* ("Remarks and Admonitions"), runs roughly like this. Everything we observe is contingent. Even an infinite collection of contingent things is itself contingent, so it needs a cause outside itself. Therefore there must be a Necessary Existent. Ibn Sina then argued that this being must be one, simple, immaterial and good. The argument became known as the Proof of the Truthful (*burhan al-siddiqin*). For a scholarly study, see Toby Mayer, "Ibn Sina's Burhan al-Siddiqin," *Journal of Islamic Studies* 12, no. 1 (2001).

**Main objections.** In his 1948 BBC radio debate with Frederick Copleston, Bertrand Russell replied, "the universe is just there, and that's all," treating it as a brute fact needing no explanation. Russell also accused the argument of a fallacy of composition: every human has a mother, but the human race does not. Defenders answer that a whole made only of dependent parts is still dependent. Within Islam, al-Ghazali objected less to the logic than to Ibn Sina's picture of God, which he thought too impersonal.

## Fine-tuning and design: proof of God in nature

Several basic numbers in physics, such as the strength of gravity and the cosmological constant, appear to fall within narrow ranges that allow stars, chemistry and life to exist. Geraint Lewis and Luke Barnes survey this evidence in *A Fortunate Universe: Life in a Finely Tuned Cosmos* (Cambridge University Press, 2016).

The book is a useful example of fair disagreement: both authors accept that fine-tuning is real, but Barnes finds a Creator the better explanation while Lewis leans toward a multiverse. The Quran describes creation as made "with [precise] determination" ([Quran 25:2](/surah/al-furqan/2)) and invites people to look at living things, "how they are created" ([Quran 88:17](/surah/al-ghashiyah/17)).

**Main objections.** David Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779) argued that design arguments cannot tell us much about the designer. Today the main replies are the multiverse (if many universes exist, some will suit life) and the anthropic principle (we could only observe a universe that permits observers). Critics also note that physics may one day explain why the constants take their values.

## Consciousness

Physics describes matter in terms of mass, charge and motion. Yet you also have experiences: the redness of red, the feeling of grief. In "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (*Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 1995), David Chalmers called the question of why physical processes produce experience at all "the hard problem."

Chalmers himself does not argue for God. But some philosophers contend that a mind-first universe explains consciousness better than a matter-first one. The Quran treats the soul as beyond full human grasp: "The soul is of the affair of my Lord. And mankind have not been given of knowledge except a little" ([Quran 17:85](/surah/al-isra/85)).

**Main objections.** Physicalists such as Daniel Dennett argue that the hard problem is a confusion that will dissolve as neuroscience progresses. Even those who take it seriously may prefer non-theistic answers, such as panpsychism.

## Objective morality

Most people believe some things are really wrong, not just unpopular: torturing a child for fun, for example. The moral argument asks what grounds such facts. If there is no God, the argument runs, moral values are human preferences or evolutionary byproducts and lose their binding force. The Quran describes the soul as created with an inner sense of right and wrong, "inspired [with discernment of] its wickedness and its righteousness" ([Quran 91:8](/surah/ash-shams/8)).

**Main objections.** The oldest is Plato's Euthyphro dilemma: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? Secular philosophers also defend moral realism without God, and evolutionary accounts explain why humans developed moral intuitions. Muslim theologians debated a version of the same question for centuries: the Mu'tazili school held that reason can know good and evil independently, while the Ash'ari school grounded moral value in God's command.

## Fitrah: is belief in God natural?

The Quran teaches that every person is born with a natural disposition, **fitrah** (*fitrah*, فطرة), that recognizes God:

> So direct your face toward the religion, inclining to truth. [Adhere to] the fitrah of Allah upon which He has created [all] people. No change should there be in the creation of Allah. That is the correct religion, but most of the people do not know.
>
> — [Quran 30:30](/surah/ar-rum/30)

The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) said "every child is born" on this natural faith, and it is the parents who change it (Sahih al-Bukhari 1385). Our guide to [fitrah and human nature](/learn/fitrah-human-nature-in-islam) explores this in depth.

The cognitive science of religion offers a striking parallel. In *Born Believers: The Science of Children's Religious Belief* (Free Press, 2012), Justin Barrett argues that young children's minds easily accept the idea of a super-knowing, immortal being. Oxford's Cognition, Religion and Theology Project, led by Barrett with Roger Trigg, reported in 2011 on studies across 20 countries suggesting humans are naturally predisposed to believe in gods and an afterlife.

**Main objections.** Barrett himself cautioned, in the project's 2011 announcement, that "just because we find it easier to think in a particular way does not mean that it is true in fact." Critics go further, arguing that these findings explain religion as a byproduct of mental tools such as over-sensitive agency detection, which would undercut belief rather than support it. Muslims reply that a natural inclination is what one would expect if fitrah is real; the data alone cannot decide between the two readings.

## Common objections to belief in God

Beyond the replies to each argument, three objections come up constantly.

### "Who created God?"

If everything needs a cause, what caused God? The arguments above do not claim that everything needs a cause, only that things which begin or which are contingent do. We answer this in full in [Who Created God?](/learn/who-created-god).

### "Why is there so much suffering?"

The problem of evil is often called the strongest argument against God. It deserves its own careful treatment, which you can read in [Why Does God Allow Suffering?](/learn/why-does-god-allow-suffering).

### "Isn't this just God of the gaps?"

The worry is that God is invoked wherever science has not yet reached. The kalam and contingency arguments are philosophical, not gaps in data: they ask why anything exists at all, a question science presupposes rather than answers. Design arguments are more exposed to this objection, so careful defenders frame them as inference to the best explanation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there proof that God exists?

There is no proof in the sense of a mathematical demonstration that compels everyone. There are philosophical arguments, including the kalam cosmological argument, Ibn Sina's contingency argument, fine-tuning and the moral argument, that many find persuasive together. Islam presents these alongside the "signs" in nature and the human conscience, while respecting that belief must be freely chosen.

### Is there proof of God in science?

Science studies how the natural world works and does not test claims about a being outside nature. However, some scientific findings feed into philosophical arguments: Big Bang cosmology is used to support the universe having a beginning, and the fine-tuning of physical constants is used in design arguments. Scientists disagree about what these findings imply, as Lewis and Barnes's *A Fortunate Universe* shows.

### What is the logical proof of God?

The most discussed logical arguments are the kalam and contingency arguments. The kalam argument says whatever begins to exist has a cause and the universe began, so it has a cause. The contingency argument says dependent things require a being that exists necessarily. Both come from medieval Islamic philosophy and remain debated in modern philosophy of religion.

### What is the cosmological proof of God?

The cosmological argument reasons from the existence of the universe to a first cause. Its kalam form, stated by al-Ghazali in *al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad* and revived by William Lane Craig in 1979, argues that the universe began to exist and so has a cause. Critics question whether ordinary causation applies to the universe as a whole.

### What was Avicenna's proof of God?

Ibn Sina (Avicenna, d. 1037) argued that everything in the world is contingent, meaning it could fail to exist and depends on something else. A chain of contingent things, even an infinite one, cannot explain itself, so there must be a Necessary Existent that exists by its own nature. It is known as the Proof of the Truthful (*burhan al-siddiqin*).

### What is the proof of God in nature?

The Quran points to order in nature, the alternation of night and day and the creation of living things as "signs" for people who reflect (Quran 3:190). In philosophy, this becomes the design argument, today usually framed around the fine-tuning of physical constants for life. Its main rivals are the multiverse hypothesis and the anthropic principle.

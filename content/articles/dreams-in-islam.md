Islamic dream interpretation begins with a simple rule from the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him): dreams are of three types. A good dream is glad tidings from God, a disturbing dream is from Satan, and many dreams are just the mind replaying its own thoughts. Only the first kind carries meaning, and even then it is interpreted with caution.

Dreams matter in Islam. Prophets received guidance through them, and the Quran tells several dream stories in detail. Yet the Prophet also taught Muslims how to shrug off bad dreams and warned against lying about them. This guide covers the three types, what to do after a good or bad dream, the dreams in the story of Yusuf, Ibn Sirin and the famous dream books, and why a dream dictionary is the wrong tool.

## Key takeaways

- The Prophet said dreams are of three types: good tidings from God, sorrowful dreams from Satan, and the suggestions of a person's own mind, as recorded in Sahih Muslim 2263.
- After a good dream, the Prophet said to thank God and share it only with someone you love; after a bad one, to seek refuge in God and tell no one (Sahih al-Bukhari 6985 and 7044).
- The Prophet said, "Whoever has seen me in a dream, then no doubt, he has seen me, for Satan cannot imitate my shape" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6994).
- Surah Yusuf contains three dream narratives, including Yusuf's childhood vision of eleven stars, the sun and the moon ([Quran 12:4](/surah/yusuf/4)).
- Ibn Sirin (died 110 AH, around 728–729 CE) was famous as a dream interpreter, but the popular dream dictionary under his name is of disputed authorship.

## What are the three types of dreams in Islam?

The core text is a hadith narrated by Abu Hurayrah in Sahih Muslim 2263. The Prophet said that "dreams are of three types: one good dream which is a sort of good tidings from Allah; the evil dream which causes pain is from the satan; and the third one is a suggestion of one's own mind."

A shorter version appears in Sahih al-Bukhari 7017, but there the three-type list is given as a saying of the early scholar Muhammad ibn Sirin, introduced with "it used to be said." In Muslim's version it is part of the Prophet's words, with the narrator unsure only about a later line on symbols. Both versions agree on the three categories, which scholars have used ever since.

| Type | Arabic term | Source | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| True or good dream | *ru'ya* (رؤيا) | From God, as glad tidings | Thank God, share only with someone who loves you |
| Bad or frightening dream | *hulm* (حلم) | From Satan, to cause distress | Seek refuge in God, spit lightly to the left, turn over, tell no one |
| Everyday dream | *hadith an-nafs* | The mind's own thoughts and worries | Nothing; it carries no message |

Most dreams fall into the third category. Scholars point out that if you spent the day worrying about an exam, a dream about that exam is just your mind at work, not a sign.

## How important are true dreams?

The Prophet described a good dream of a righteous person as "one of forty-six parts of prophetism" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6983). Sahih Muslim 2263 gives the number as forty-five; scholars explain that the narrations differ in wording and agree in meaning. The point is that a true dream is a small trace of the way God communicates with prophets, not that the dreamer becomes a prophet.

Aisha reported that the Prophet's revelation began with true dreams that came out "like bright daylight," recorded at the very opening of Sahih al-Bukhari. The Quran also refers to his dreams, such as the vision of entering Makkah in safety:

> Certainly has Allah showed to His Messenger the vision in truth. You will surely enter al-Masjid al-Haram, if Allah wills, in safety, with your heads shaved and [hair] shortened, not fearing [anyone]. He knew what you did not know and has arranged before that a conquest near [at hand].
>
> — [Quran 48:27](/surah/al-fath/27)

Sahih Muslim 2263 adds that "the truest vision will be of one who is himself the most truthful in speech." Honesty while awake is linked to clarity while asleep.

## What should you do after a good or bad dream?

The Prophet gave practical steps for both, and they are among the clearest instructions on dreams in the hadith.

### After a good dream

In Sahih al-Bukhari 6985, the Prophet said that if you see a dream you like, it is from God, so "he should thank Allah for it and narrate it to others." Sahih al-Bukhari 7044 narrows this: share it only "to the one whom he loves." A good dream told to someone envious can invite resentment, as Yusuf's father warned him ([Quran 12:5](/surah/yusuf/5)).

### After a bad dream or nightmare

The hadith collect five steps. If you see a dream you dislike:

1. Seek refuge in God from Satan and from the dream's evil (Sahih al-Bukhari 6986).
2. Spit lightly to your left three times (Sahih Muslim 2262).
3. Turn over from the side you were sleeping on (Sahih Muslim 2262).
4. Do not tell anyone about it (Sahih al-Bukhari 6985).
5. If you wish, get up and pray (Sahih Muslim 2263).

The Prophet promised that if you do this, "it will not harm him" (Sahih al-Bukhari 7044). In Sahih Muslim 2268, he called a bad dream "a sort of vain sport of devil in the state of sleep." For anyone troubled by recurring nightmares, this is meant as reassurance: the dream has no authority over your life.

The early Muslim Abu Salamah said he used to see dreams that made him sick, until he heard this teaching from the companion Abu Qatadah (Sahih al-Bukhari 7044). People with persistent nightmares, trauma or sleep disorders should still seek medical or psychological help, which Islam fully supports.

## What does it mean to see the Prophet in a dream?

The Prophet said, "Whoever has seen me in a dream, then no doubt, he has seen me, for Satan cannot imitate my shape" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6994; also Sahih Muslim 2266). Muslims treat such a dream as a great blessing.

Scholars add two cautions. First, many say the hadith applies when he is seen in his known description, as recorded in the books of his features. Second, a dream cannot add to or change the religion. Islamic law is complete, so no ruling, practice or claim to authority can be taken from a dream. Anyone who claims a dream has given them special religious status should be treated with suspicion.

The Prophet also warned against inventing dreams. Sahih al-Bukhari 7042 records that whoever claims to have seen a dream they did not see will be made, on the Day of Judgement, to tie a knot between two grains of barley, an impossible task.

## Prophet Yusuf's dreams: the Quran's dream story

[Surah Yusuf](/surah/yusuf), chapter 12, is the Quran's fullest treatment of dreams. It opens with a young Yusuf telling his father his vision:

> [Of these stories mention] when Joseph said to his father, "O my father, indeed I have seen [in a dream] eleven stars and the sun and the moon; I saw them prostrating to me."
>
> — [Quran 12:4](/surah/yusuf/4)

The surah then tells of two dreams from Yusuf's fellow prisoners ([12:36](/surah/yusuf/36)) and the king's dream of seven fat cows eaten by seven lean ones ([12:43](/surah/yusuf/43)). The king's advisers dismissed it as "a mixture of false dreams" ([12:44](/surah/yusuf/44)), but Yusuf read it as seven years of plenty followed by seven of famine.

Three lessons stand out. Interpretation is described as knowledge God teaches ([12:6](/surah/yusuf/6)), not a technique anyone can master from a list. The dream's fulfillment took decades. And when it came, Yusuf credited God: "My Lord has made it reality" ([12:100](/surah/yusuf/100)).

Other prophetic dreams appear too. Abraham's dream of sacrificing his son ([Quran 37:102](/surah/as-saffat/102)) shows that a prophet's dream is a form of revelation. Ordinary people's dreams do not carry that status.

## Ibn Sirin and Islamic dream interpretation books

**Muhammad ibn Sirin** was a scholar of Basra in the generation after the companions, who died in 110 AH (around 728–729 CE). He narrated hadith, including on dreams, and was renowned as an interpreter. The comment on dream symbols in Sahih al-Bukhari 7017, that seeing fetters on the feet means firmness in religion, comes from his circle.

The well-known dream dictionary sold as "Ibn Sirin's Dream Interpretation" is another matter. Its attribution to him is disputed, and many historians regard the dream manuals carrying his name as later compilations. Treat any specific entry from them as the opinion of an unknown author, not as Ibn Sirin's teaching or the Prophet's.

## Why dream dictionaries mislead

Search for "Islamic dream interpretation" and most results are dictionaries that match symbols to meanings: snakes mean enemies, teeth falling out means a death in the family. There are good reasons to be wary of them.

- **Most dreams mean nothing.** The hadith puts everyday mental replays and Satanic disturbances outside interpretation entirely.
- **Symbols depend on the person.** Classical interpreters asked about the dreamer's life, faith and circumstances. Yusuf read the king's cows in the context of Egypt's harvests.
- **Bad dreams should not be told.** A dictionary invites people to look up and dwell on frightening dreams, which is the opposite of the Prophet's instruction.
- **False certainty causes harm.** Reading a dream as proof that someone is cursed, betrayed or about to die damages trust and peace of mind.

If a good dream seems significant, the Prophet's advice is to share it with someone who loves you and, if needed, a knowledgeable, sincere person. Then leave it with God.

## Do you need a dream after istikhara?

No. In the hadith of the istikhara prayer, Jabir reports that the Prophet taught it "for each and every matter" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6382), and the text makes no mention of dreams. The answer to istikhara is usually seen in how events unfold: the matter becomes easy, or obstacles appear.

A dream after istikhara may reassure you, but its absence does not mean anything. Our guide to [the istikhara prayer](/learn/istikhara-prayer) explains how to pray it and what to expect.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the three types of dreams in Islam?

According to Sahih Muslim 2263, the Prophet said dreams are of three types: a good dream that is glad tidings from God, a distressing dream from Satan, and a dream arising from a person's own thoughts. Only the first may carry meaning. Most dreams belong to the third category and need no interpretation at all.

### What should I do after a bad dream in Islam?

Seek refuge in God from Satan, spit lightly to your left three times, turn onto your other side, and do not tell anyone about it. You may also get up and pray. These steps come from Sahih al-Bukhari 6985 and 7044 and Sahih Muslim 2262 and 2263, and the Prophet said that the dream will then not harm you.

### What does it mean to see the Prophet in a dream?

The Prophet said that whoever sees him in a dream has truly seen him, because Satan cannot take his form (Sahih al-Bukhari 6994). Many scholars say this applies when he appears in his known description. Such a dream is a blessing, but it cannot create new religious rulings or give anyone special authority.

### Is Ibn Sirin's dream interpretation book authentic?

Muhammad ibn Sirin (died 110 AH) was a respected scholar of Basra and a famous dream interpreter, but the popular dream dictionary under his name is of disputed authorship. Many historians regard it as a later compilation. Its entries should not be treated as Ibn Sirin's own teaching, still less as prophetic guidance.

### Are dreams from Allah?

Some are. The Prophet said good dreams are from God (Sahih al-Bukhari 6985) and described a true dream of a righteous person as one of forty-six parts of prophethood (Sahih al-Bukhari 6983). Bad dreams are from Satan, and many dreams are just the mind's own thoughts. Only prophets' dreams count as revelation.

### Should I tell people about my dreams?

Share a good dream only with someone who loves you, as the Prophet advised in Sahih al-Bukhari 7044, because envy can follow. Do not tell anyone about a bad dream. Never invent or exaggerate a dream: Sahih al-Bukhari 7042 warns of a severe consequence for anyone who claims to have seen a dream they did not see.

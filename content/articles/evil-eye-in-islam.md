In Islam, the evil eye (*al-'ayn*) is real: the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) said "the evil eye is a fact," as recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim. It is harm that can follow an admiring or envious look, but it only touches anyone by God's permission. Protection comes through remembrance of God, not charms or beads.

The idea of a harmful gaze is found in many cultures, from the Mediterranean to South Asia. Islam accepts that the evil eye exists while stripping it of superstition. This guide covers what the Quran and authentic hadith say, the duas the Prophet used, what ruqyah is, why amulets are rejected, and how to keep the subject in proportion.

## Key takeaways

- The Prophet said "The evil eye is a fact" (*al-'aynu haqq*), a statement recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari 5740 and Sahih Muslim 2188.
- Classical commentators, including Ibn Abbas as cited in Tafsir Ibn Kathir, read [Quran 68:51](/surah/al-qalam/51) as a reference to the evil eye.
- The Prophet taught that when you see something you admire, you should pray for blessing on it, according to a hadith in Sunan Ibn Majah 3509 graded authentic.
- The Prophet recited the last two surahs of the Quran, al-Falaq and an-Nas, for protection and healing, as Aisha reports in Sahih al-Bukhari 5016.
- Islam permits ruqyah (healing recitation) "as long as there is no polytheism in it" (Sahih Muslim 2200), but treats charms and amulets as forbidden.

## What is the evil eye in Islam?

**The evil eye** (*al-'ayn*, العين) is harm that reaches a person, a child, a possession or a blessing after someone looks at it with admiration or envy. Scholars explain that the look itself has no independent power. God allows the harm to occur, just as He allows fire to burn, so the evil eye sits inside divine decree rather than outside it.

It is linked to, but not identical with, **envy** (*hasad*). Envy is wishing that another person would lose a blessing. The evil eye can come from envy, but scholars note it can also come from simple admiration, even from a loving parent who praises a child without remembering God. That is why the Islamic remedy focuses on the person looking as much as the person looked at.

## Is the evil eye real? What the Quran and hadith say

The clearest evidence is in the hadith. In Sahih Muslim 2188, Ibn Abbas reports the Prophet saying that the evil eye is a fact, and that "if anything would precede the destiny it would be the influence of an evil eye." The wording is careful: nothing actually precedes destiny, but the evil eye comes close in its effect. Sahih al-Bukhari 5740 records the same short statement, "The effect of an evil eye is a fact."

The Quran also points toward it. Describing the hostility of the Prophet's opponents, God says:

> And indeed, those who disbelieve would almost make you slip with their eyes when they hear the message, and they say, "Indeed, he is mad."
>
> — [Quran 68:51](/surah/al-qalam/51)

Ibn Kathir, citing Ibn Abbas and Mujahid, explains "make you slip with their eyes" as affecting the Prophet with their gaze, and adds that only God's protection prevented it. He opens the next section of his commentary with the heading "The Effect of the Evil Eye is Real."

A second Quranic passage comes from the story of Prophet Yusuf (Joseph). When his father Ya'qub sent his sons into Egypt, he told them to enter by different gates. Ibn Kathir reports that Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, Qatadah and others understood this as fear of the evil eye, because the brothers were striking to look at. Notice how Ya'qub immediately adds that his precaution cannot change what God decrees:

> And he said, "O my sons, do not enter from one gate but enter from different gates; and I cannot avail you against [the decree of] Allah at all. The decision is only for Allah; upon Him I have relied, and upon Him let those who would rely [indeed] rely."
>
> — [Quran 12:67](/surah/yusuf/67)

That balance, taking sensible precautions while trusting God, is the model for the whole topic.

## How do you protect yourself from the evil eye?

Protection in Islam rests on remembrance of God (*dhikr*), recitation of the Quran and reliance on Him. None of it needs special objects or rituals.

### Recite al-Falaq, an-Nas and Ayatul Kursi

[Surah al-Falaq](/surah/al-falaq) ends by seeking refuge "from the evil of an envier when he envies," which speaks directly to envy and the evil eye:

> Say, "I seek refuge in the Lord of daybreak From the evil of that which He created And from the evil of darkness when it settles And from the evil of the blowers in knots And from the evil of an envier when he envies."
>
> — [Quran 113:1–5](/surah/al-falaq/1)

Aisha reports in Sahih al-Bukhari 5016 that whenever the Prophet fell ill he recited the *Mu'awwidhat* (al-Falaq and an-Nas) and blew over his body. Many Muslims recite these with Surah al-Ikhlas each morning and evening and before sleep; our guide to the [four Quls](/learn/four-quls) explains their meaning. [Ayatul Kursi](/learn/ayatul-kursi), recited before sleep, is another protection taught in Sahih al-Bukhari.

### The Prophet's dua for protection from the evil eye

Ibn Abbas reports in Sahih al-Bukhari 3371 that the Prophet sought protection for his grandsons al-Hasan and al-Husayn with words that, he said, Abraham had used for Ishmael and Isaac:

> أَعُوذُ بِكَلِمَاتِ اللَّهِ التَّامَّةِ مِنْ كُلِّ شَيْطَانٍ وَهَامَّةٍ، وَمِنْ كُلِّ عَيْنٍ لاَمَّةٍ
>
> *A'udhu bi kalimatillahit-tammati min kulli shaytanin wa hammah, wa min kulli 'aynin lammah.*
>
> "I seek refuge in the perfect words of Allah from every devil and every poisonous creature, and from every harmful, envious eye."

Parents often say this over their children. When reciting it for two children, the Prophet's wording began *u'idhukuma* ("I seek refuge for you both"), so the opening word changes to fit who is being protected.

### The ruqyah of Jibril

Sahih Muslim 2186 records that the angel Jibril came to the Prophet when he was ill and recited this over him:

> بِاسْمِ اللَّهِ أَرْقِيكَ مِنْ كُلِّ شَىْءٍ يُؤْذِيكَ مِنْ شَرِّ كُلِّ نَفْسٍ أَوْ عَيْنِ حَاسِدٍ اللَّهُ يَشْفِيكَ بِاسْمِ اللَّهِ أَرْقِيكَ
>
> *Bismillahi arqika, min kulli shay'in yu'dhika, min sharri kulli nafsin aw 'ayni hasidin, Allahu yashfika, bismillahi arqika.*
>
> "In the name of Allah I perform ruqyah for you, from everything that harms you, from the evil of every soul or envious eye. May Allah heal you. In the name of Allah I perform ruqyah for you."

This is one of the most widely used duas for the evil eye, and it can be read over someone who is unwell.

## Why do Muslims say mashallah?

The most practical protection is on the side of the person looking. In Sunan Ibn Majah 3509 (graded sahih), the companion Amir ibn Rabi'ah saw Sahl ibn Hunayf bathing and exclaimed that he had never seen such fine skin. Sahl collapsed. The Prophet asked, "Why would anyone of you kill his brother? If he sees something that he likes, then let him pray for blessing for him." He then told Amir to wash, and the water was poured over Sahl.

From this, Muslims are taught to respond to admiration by mentioning God: *barakallahu fik* ("may God bless you") or *Allahumma barik 'alayh* ("O God, bless it"). Many also say *mashallah*, drawing on [Quran 18:39](/surah/al-kahf/39), where a believer rebukes his proud companion: "why did you, when you entered your garden, not say, 'What Allah willed [has occurred]; there is no power except in Allah'?" Our guide to [mashallah meaning](/learn/mashallah-meaning) covers when to say it and how it differs from *tabarakallah*.

## What is ruqyah for the evil eye?

**Ruqyah** (*ruqyah*, رقية) is healing through recitation: reading Quranic verses and prophetic duas over a sick person, often with light blowing. In Sahih al-Bukhari 5738, Aisha says the Prophet instructed that ruqyah be done for the evil eye.

The Prophet set one clear condition. When companions asked about the incantations they had used before Islam, he said, "There is no harm in the invocation as long as there is no polytheism in it" (Sahih Muslim 2200). Scholars derive three common conditions from this: it uses God's words or names, it is understandable, and the reciter believes that healing comes from God, not from the words themselves.

Ruqyah does not require a specialist. Any Muslim can recite al-Fatihah, Ayatul Kursi and the Quls over themselves or a family member.

## Is the evil eye bracelet haram?

The blue glass *nazar* bead, the hand-shaped *hamsa* and similar evil eye bracelets are common across Turkey, the Middle East and South Asia. The majority of scholars hold that wearing them for protection is not allowed, because they ask an object to do what only God does.

The evidence is a hadith in Sunan Abi Dawud 3883, graded authentic by al-Albani, in which Abdullah ibn Mas'ud heard the Prophet say that "spells, charms and love-potions are polytheism." The Arabic word for charms here, *tama'im*, refers to amulets hung on people or animals to ward off the eye. Ibn Mas'ud then taught a dua of the Prophet in its place.

| Practice | Islamic ruling | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reciting al-Falaq, an-Nas, Ayatul Kursi | Encouraged | Taught and practised by the Prophet (Bukhari 5016) |
| Saying mashallah or barakallah when admiring | Encouraged | Prophet's instruction to pray for blessing (Ibn Majah 3509) |
| Ruqyah with Quran and prophetic duas | Permitted and recommended | "No harm as long as there is no polytheism" (Muslim 2200) |
| Washing by the person who gave the eye | Permitted | Prophetic instruction (Muslim 2188, Ibn Majah 3509) |
| Nazar bead, hamsa or evil eye bracelet | Not permitted (majority view) | Amulets are classed as polytheism (Abu Dawud 3883) |
| Amulets containing Quranic verses | Disputed | Some early scholars allowed them; others forbade them to block misuse |

On the last row, scholars have genuinely differed. Some early authorities permitted wearing written Quranic verses, while others, including the students of Ibn Mas'ud, disallowed even these. Many contemporary scholars favor the stricter view because such items are easily treated as charms.

## What are the signs of the evil eye?

There is no list of signs in the Quran or authentic hadith. Popular lists, such as sudden tiredness, yawning, loss of appetite or a run of bad luck, come from folk practice and modern ruqyah literature, not from the Prophet.

This matters, because these "signs" also describe ordinary illness, stress and depression. Islam teaches that seeking medical treatment is part of trusting God, not a lack of faith. The sensible approach is to see a doctor for physical or mental symptoms, keep up your daily remembrance and ruqyah, and avoid diagnosing family members or neighbors as the cause.

## Keeping the evil eye in proportion

Blaming every setback on the evil eye can do real harm. It breeds suspicion toward relatives and friends, it can keep people from medical care, and it can push them toward paid "healers" who exploit fear. Scholars warn against all three.

The Quran's answer to fear is trust in God. Ya'qub took a precaution and then said "upon Him I have relied." Muslims believe that whatever befalls them is within God's knowledge and wisdom, a theme explored in our guide to [why God allows suffering](/learn/why-does-god-allow-suffering). Envy is also a matter of the heart: the cure for being an envier is to be glad for others and ask God to bless what they have.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the evil eye real in Islam?

Yes. The Prophet Muhammad said "the evil eye is a fact," as recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari 5740 and Sahih Muslim 2188. Islam teaches that it can only harm by God's permission, like any other cause in the world. Muslims are encouraged to protect themselves through remembrance of God and recitation of the Quran, not through amulets or superstition.

### What is the dua for protection from the evil eye?

A well-known dua is the one the Prophet used for his grandsons: *A'udhu bi kalimatillahit-tammati min kulli shaytanin wa hammah, wa min kulli 'aynin lammah*, "I seek refuge in the perfect words of Allah from every devil, every poisonous creature and every harmful eye" (Sahih al-Bukhari 3371). Reciting Surah al-Falaq, Surah an-Nas and Ayatul Kursi is also a central protection.

### Is wearing an evil eye bracelet haram?

The majority of scholars say it is not permitted to wear a nazar bead, hamsa or evil eye bracelet for protection. A hadith in Sunan Abi Dawud 3883 classes charms and amulets as polytheism, because they attribute protective power to an object rather than to God. Wearing similar jewelry purely as decoration, with no protective belief, is a separate question on which scholars advise caution.

### What are the signs of the evil eye?

No authentic hadith lists signs of the evil eye. The symptoms in popular lists, such as fatigue, sudden illness or misfortune, overlap with ordinary medical and emotional conditions. Scholars advise seeing a doctor, continuing daily remembrance and ruqyah, and avoiding accusing other people. Treating symptoms medically is fully consistent with trusting God.

### How do you remove the evil eye in Islam?

The Prophet taught two remedies. First, ruqyah: reciting al-Fatihah, Ayatul Kursi, al-Falaq, an-Nas and prophetic duas over the affected person (Sahih al-Bukhari 5738). Second, if the person who gave the eye is known, they are asked to wash and the water is poured over the one affected, as in Sahih Muslim 2188 and Sunan Ibn Majah 3509.

### Why do Muslims say mashallah to avoid the evil eye?

Saying *mashallah* ("what God has willed") or *barakallah* ("may God bless") turns admiration into remembrance of God. The Prophet told a companion whose admiring look harmed another to "pray for blessing" when he saw something he liked (Sunan Ibn Majah 3509). The phrase also echoes Quran 18:39, which rebukes a man for admiring his garden without mentioning God.

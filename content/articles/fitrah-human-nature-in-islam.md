Fitrah is the Islamic term for the original, uncorrupted nature with which God creates every human being. It includes an innate inclination to recognize one Creator and to love what is good. The Quran calls it "the fitrah of Allah upon which He has created [all] people" (30:30), and the Prophet taught that every child is born upon it.

The idea of fitrah answers an old question: is belief in God learned, or is it natural? Islam's answer is that it is natural, and that upbringing and culture either nurture it or cover it up. This guide explains the meaning of fitrah, the verses and hadith behind it, what modern cognitive science has found about children and belief, and why many converts to Islam call themselves "reverts."

## Key takeaways

- The Quran names fitrah directly: "the fitrah of Allah upon which He has created [all] people. No change should there be in the creation of Allah" ([Quran 30:30](/surah/ar-rum/30)).
- The Prophet Muhammad said every child is born upon fitrah, and that parents make the child a Jew, Christian or Magian, according to Sahih al-Bukhari 1385 and Sahih Muslim 2658.
- In a hadith qudsi in Sahih Muslim 2865, God says He created His servants with a natural inclination to worship Him, and devils turned them away.
- The Quran describes a primordial covenant in which all the descendants of Adam testified, "Yes," to God being their Lord ([Quran 7:172](/surah/al-araf/172)).
- A three-year Oxford-led research project reported in 2011 that humans are naturally predisposed to believe in gods and an afterlife, while stressing this does not prove God exists.

## What does fitrah mean?

**Fitrah** (*fitrah*, فطرة) comes from the Arabic root f-t-r, meaning to split open, originate or create something for the first time. The Quran opens Surah Fatir by calling God *fatir al-samawati wal-ard*, translated "Creator of the heavens and the earth" ([Quran 35:1](/surah/fatir/1)). Fitrah is therefore the original pattern of creation: the way a human being is made before anything is added.

In Islamic teaching, fitrah includes three things:

1. An innate awareness that there is a Creator, and an inclination to worship Him alone.
2. A basic moral sense: a natural attraction to truth, justice and kindness, and an aversion to cruelty and lies.
3. Healthy natural instincts, such as the desire for family and cleanliness, which Islamic law is designed to work with rather than against.

Islam does not teach original sin. Humans are born pure, with a nature already oriented toward God. The Quran says, "We have certainly created man in the best of stature" ([Quran 95:4](/surah/at-tin/4)).

## What does Quran 30:30 say about fitrah?

The key verse is in Surah ar-Rum:

> So direct your face toward the religion, inclining to truth. [Adhere to] the fitrah of Allah upon which He has created [all] people. No change should there be in the creation of Allah. That is the correct religion, but most of the people do not know.
>
> — [Quran 30:30](/surah/ar-rum/30)

In Arabic the central phrase reads *fitrat Allahi allati fatara al-nasa 'alayha* (فِطۡرَتَ ٱللَّهِ ٱلَّتِي فَطَرَ ٱلنَّاسَ عَلَيۡهَا). Notice three points. The verse says fitrah applies to *all* people, not only Muslims. It identifies fitrah with "the correct religion," so Islam presents itself as the religion that matches human nature. And it says most people "do not know," meaning fitrah can be present but unrecognized.

The phrase "no change should there be in the creation of Allah" has been read in two ways by commentators: as a statement that the fitrah itself is never fully erased, and as a command not to corrupt it.

## Is every child born Muslim?

This popular phrase comes from a hadith. In Sahih al-Bukhari 1385, the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) said:

> "Every child is born with a true faith of Islam (i.e. to worship none but Allah Alone) and his parents convert him to Judaism or Christianity or Magianism, as an animal delivers a perfect baby animal. Do you find it mutilated?"

The words in brackets are the translator's explanation. The Arabic says every child is born "upon the fitrah." In a version in Sahih Muslim (2658), the narrator Abu Hurayrah followed the hadith by reciting Quran 30:30, linking the two directly.

The image is of a newborn animal, whole and unmarked. Any "mutilation" comes later, from outside. In the same way, children are born with an intact orientation toward God, and their environment shapes how they express it.

Scholars explain that "born Muslim" here means born with the natural disposition toward submitting to God, not born with knowledge of Islamic law or rituals. Imam al-Bukhari placed this hadith in a chapter on the children of non-Muslims who die young, and many scholars cite it to argue that such children are not held to account.

A related hadith qudsi in Sahih Muslim (2865) states it from God's side: "I have created My servants as one having a natural inclination to the worship of Allah but it is Satan who turns them away from the right religion."

## What is the covenant of Quran 7:172?

The Quran describes an event before earthly life, in which all of Adam's descendants were brought forth and asked a single question:

> And [mention] when your Lord took from the children of Adam - from their loins - their descendants and made them testify of themselves, [saying to them], "Am I not your Lord?" They said, "Yes, we have testified." [This] - lest you should say on the day of Resurrection, "Indeed, we were of this unaware."
>
> — [Quran 7:172](/surah/al-araf/172)

This is known as the primordial covenant, or *mithaq*. The next verse adds that no one will be able to excuse themselves by saying they only followed their ancestors ([Quran 7:173](/surah/al-araf/173)). Many scholars connect this covenant to fitrah: humans do not remember the event, but its trace remains as an inner recognition of God.

## Fitrah and the moral sense

The Quran also describes an inner moral compass:

> And [by] the soul and He who proportioned it And inspired it [with discernment of] its wickedness and its righteousness, He has succeeded who purifies it, And he has failed who instills it [with corruption].
>
> — [Quran 91:7–10](/surah/ash-shams/7)

This is fitrah's ethical side. People across cultures recognize that betrayal is wrong and that kindness to the weak is good, even when they disagree on details. Islam explains this shared moral sense as part of how God made us, and treats revelation as confirming and completing it.

## What does the cognitive science of religion say?

Since the 1990s, a field called the **cognitive science of religion** has studied why religious belief is so widespread. Its findings are often discussed alongside the Islamic idea of fitrah, though they must be read with care.

| Finding | Researcher and source | What it suggests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Young children tend to see natural things as made for a purpose | Deborah Kelemen, "Are Children 'Intuitive Theists'?", *Psychological Science* (2004) | Children may be naturally receptive to the idea of a designer |
| Belief in gods appears to arise naturally from how children's minds develop | Justin L. Barrett, *Born Believers: The Science of Children's Religious Belief* (2012) | Belief is not only taught; minds are primed for it |
| Three-year-olds tend to think both their mother and God would know hidden contents; by four they understand their mother can be wrong | Oxford-led Cognition, Religion and Theology Project (reported 2011) | Children find superhuman knowledge easy to accept |
| Humans across 20 countries are "predisposed" to believe in gods and an afterlife | Same project: 57 researchers, over 40 studies | Religious belief is a common feature of human nature |

Kelemen's paper reviewed research showing that young children reason about the natural world in terms of purpose, and asked whether they are best described as "intuitive theists" ([Psychological Science, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0956-7976.2004.00672.x)). Barrett, who led the Oxford project described below, argued in *Born Believers* that belief in gods is a natural product of normal child development.

The Oxford-led project, a £1.9 million effort involving 57 researchers in 20 countries, concluded that "both theology and atheism are reasoned responses to what is a basic impulse of the human mind" ([ScienceDaily, 2011](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/07/110714103828.htm)).

### A note of caution

These findings do not prove that God exists, and the researchers say so. Barrett stated plainly that the project "does not set out to prove god or gods exist." Other scholars read the same data naturalistically, as a by-product of mental tools that evolved for other purposes. What the research does show is consistent with the Islamic claim: belief in a Creator comes easily to human minds and does not have to be forced in from outside. ## Why do some converts say "revert" instead of "convert"?

Because of fitrah, many people who embrace Islam describe themselves as **reverts** rather than converts. The idea is that they are not adopting something foreign but returning to the natural state in which they were born.

Both terms are used, and neither is required. Some Muslims prefer "convert" because it is clearer in English, or because "revert" can sound as if it dismisses a person's past. Others find "revert" meaningful because it matches their experience of coming home. Islamic law treats both the same.

## How is fitrah covered, and how is it restored?

Islam teaches that fitrah is never destroyed, but it can be buried. The Quran and hadith point to several causes: upbringing that teaches false beliefs, habitual sin that dulls the heart, constant distraction, and pride that refuses to accept truth.

Restoring it does not mean adding something new. It means removing what covers it: reflecting on creation, as the Quran repeatedly asks ([Quran 3:190](/surah/ali-imran/190)); returning to God through repentance; and worshipping Him alone, which is the core of [tawheed](/learn/what-is-tawheed). Muslims understand the prophets' role in the same way, as reminders of what people already know deep down. The Quran itself calls its message "a reminder to the worlds" ([Quran 38:87](/surah/sad/87)).

The pull toward meaning that people feel even in comfortable lives is, in this view, fitrah asking to be answered. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is the meaning of fitrah?

Fitrah means the original, natural disposition God created in every human being. It comes from an Arabic root meaning to originate or create. In Islam, it includes an innate recognition of one Creator, an inclination to worship Him, and a basic moral sense. The Quran calls it "the fitrah of Allah upon which He has created [all] people" (30:30).

### Is every child born Muslim in Islam?

According to Sahih al-Bukhari 1385 and Sahih Muslim 2658, the Prophet said every child is born upon fitrah, and parents make the child a Jew, Christian or Magian. Scholars explain this means every child is born with a natural inclination to submit to one God, not with knowledge of Islamic practice. Upbringing then shapes how that nature develops.

### Is belief in God innate?

Islam teaches that belief in God is innate, part of the fitrah every person is born with. Research in the cognitive science of religion, including work by Justin Barrett and Deborah Kelemen, suggests children find belief in a creator and superhuman minds intuitive. Researchers note this shows belief is natural, not that it is true or false.

### What is the difference between fitrah and Islam?

Fitrah is the natural disposition every human is born with, while Islam is the revealed religion that matches and completes it. Quran 30:30 links them, calling fitrah "the correct religion." In the Islamic view, fitrah gives a general awareness of God and morality, and revelation supplies the details: how to worship, what God is like, and how to live.

### Why do converts to Islam say they are reverts?

Many new Muslims say "revert" because Islam teaches that every person is born upon fitrah, a natural inclination to worship one God. Accepting Islam is therefore seen as returning to one's original nature rather than adopting something foreign. Both "convert" and "revert" are acceptable, and the choice is a personal preference.

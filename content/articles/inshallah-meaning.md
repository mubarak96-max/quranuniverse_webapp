Inshallah (*in sha' Allah*, إن شاء الله) is an Arabic phrase meaning "if God wills" or "God willing." Muslims say it whenever they speak about something they plan or hope to do in the future, following a direct instruction in the Quran (18:23-24). It expresses real intention combined with the humble admission that only God controls what happens next.

You will hear inshallah from Muslims in every language, and from many Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews too. This guide explains what the phrase means word by word, where it comes from, how to spell it, when to use it, and how to reply when someone says it to you.

## Key takeaways

- Inshallah means "if God wills" and is made of three Arabic words: *in* (if), *sha'a* (He willed) and *Allah* (God).
- The Quran tells believers never to say "I will do that tomorrow" without adding "if Allah wills" (Quran 18:23-24).
- Commentators such as Ibn Kathir link those verses to a time when revelation was delayed after the Prophet promised an answer "tomorrow" without saying inshallah.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6639 records that the Prophet said Prophet Solomon would have achieved his aim had he said "if Allah wills."
- The Prophet taught that inshallah should not be added to a dua; Sahih al-Bukhari 6339 says to ask God with determination.

## What does inshallah mean?

The phrase has three parts. *In* means "if." *Sha'a* is the past tense of the verb "to will" or "to want," so it means "has willed." *Allah* is the Arabic word for God. Put together, it reads literally as "if God has willed," and in natural English it becomes "if God wills" or "God willing."

Behind these simple words sits a core Islamic belief. Muslims hold that nothing happens outside God's will and knowledge, a belief called **qadar** (*qadar*, قدر), divine decree. Saying inshallah is a small, daily way of acknowledging that a person can plan and work hard, but the outcome belongs to God.

The Quran states this principle in several places, including:

> And you do not will except that Allah wills - Lord of the worlds.
>
> — [Quran 81:29](/surah/at-takwir/29)

So inshallah is not a way of avoiding commitment. It is a sentence of intention with God placed at its center.

## Inshallah in Arabic and how to spell it

In Arabic, inshallah is written as three separate words: إن شاء الله. Because Arabic has sounds that English letters cannot capture exactly, there is no single "correct" English spelling. The common versions are below.

| Spelling | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Inshallah | The most common everyday spelling in English |
| In sha Allah | Separates the three Arabic words; preferred by many Muslim writers |
| In sha' Allah | Adds an apostrophe for the glottal stop at the end of *sha'a* |
| Insha'Allah | A popular middle form, written as one or two words |
| Inshaallah | Seen on social media; less common in print |

### Inshallah vs in sha Allah: does the spelling matter?

Some Muslim teachers encourage writing "in sha Allah" as three words. Their reason comes from Arabic, not English. The single Arabic word **insha'** (إنشاء) means "creation" or "composition," so if someone wrote the phrase in Arabic script as إنشاء الله, it would read as "the creation of God," which is not the intended meaning.

In English letters, the issue is mostly one of clarity and habit. Any of the spellings above is understood as "God willing." Writing it as three words simply mirrors the Arabic structure more closely, which is why you will see "in sha Allah" in many Islamic books.

## Where does inshallah come from? Quran 18:23-24

The instruction to say inshallah comes from Surah al-Kahf, the eighteenth chapter of the Quran:

> And never say of anything, "Indeed, I will do that tomorrow," Except [when adding], "If Allah wills." And remember your Lord when you forget [it] and say, "Perhaps my Lord will guide me to what is nearer than this to right conduct."
>
> — [Quran 18:23–24](/surah/al-kahf/23)

### The story behind the verse

Ibn Kathir's commentary records a report from Ibn Ishaq about why these verses came down. Leaders of Quraysh in Makkah, advised by rabbis in Madinah, asked the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) three questions: about some young men of ancient times, about a great traveler, and about the soul. He replied, "I will tell you tomorrow," without saying "if Allah wills."

According to the report, no revelation came for fifteen days, and people in Makkah began to mock him. The answer then arrived in Surah al-Kahf, which tells the stories of the People of the Cave and Dhul-Qarnayn, together with this lesson about the future. Ibn Ishaq's chain for this report includes an unnamed narrator, so scholars treat its details with some caution, but the lesson of the verse is clear on its own. Our article on [Surah al-Kahf benefits](/learn/surah-al-kahf-benefits) covers the rest of the chapter.

### "Remember your Lord when you forget"

The second half of 18:24 deals with a very human problem: forgetting. Ibn Kathir notes that early scholars such as Abu al-'Aliyah and al-Hasan al-Basri understood it to mean that if you forget to say inshallah, you say it once you remember.

### Prophets who said "if Allah wills"

The Quran shows prophets and righteous people using the phrase. When Moses asked to travel with al-Khidr, he promised: "You will find me, if Allah wills, patient" ([18:69](/surah/al-kahf/69)). When Prophet Joseph welcomed his family, he said, "Enter Egypt, Allah willing, safe [and secure]" ([12:99](/surah/yusuf/99)). Even Ishmael, told of his father's dream, answered, "You will find me, if Allah wills, of the steadfast" ([37:102](/surah/as-saffat/102)).

The Quran also gives a warning example. In Surah al-Qalam, the owners of a garden swore to harvest it early in the morning "without making exception" ([68:18](/surah/al-qalam/18)), meaning without saying inshallah. They woke to find the garden destroyed.

### The hadith of Prophet Solomon

Sahih al-Bukhari 6639 records the Prophet's account of Solomon, who said he would visit his wives in one night so that each would bear a son who would strive in God's cause. His companion told him to say "if Allah wills," but he did not. Only one child was born, and incomplete. The Prophet said that if Solomon had said "if Allah wills," his wish would have been fulfilled. A parallel narration appears in Sahih al-Bukhari 5242.

## When do Muslims say inshallah?

The basic rule is simple: say it whenever you speak about the future. In practice, that covers a lot of daily life.

- **Plans and promises.** "I'll see you tomorrow, inshallah." "The exam is next week, inshallah I'll pass."
- **Hopes for others.** When visiting the sick, the Prophet used to say, "Don't worry, it will be a purification, if Allah wills" (Sahih al-Bukhari 5656).
- **Oaths.** In the Solomon hadith, the Prophet said that adding "if Allah wills" would have kept the oath from being broken, which is why jurists discuss inshallah in the rules of oaths.
- **Even certain things.** Sahih Muslim 974 records the Prophet greeting the dead at al-Baqi' cemetery with the words "God willing, we shall join you," although death itself is certain.

That last example shows that inshallah is not only about uncertainty. Even when something is sure to happen, the believer still links it to God's will. Saying it is a habit of the heart, much like the reliance on God described in our guide to [tawakkul](/learn/tawakkul-trust-in-god).

## Does "inshallah" really mean "no"?

There is a well-known joke, among Muslims as much as anyone, that inshallah can be a polite way of saying "probably not." A parent asked for a new phone might answer "inshallah," and every child knows what that can mean.

The stereotype has some truth in everyday culture, but it is a misuse of the phrase, not its meaning. In Islamic teaching, inshallah is meant to accompany a real intention. Using it to hide a refusal turns a sentence about trust in God into a way of avoiding honesty, which the Quran and the Prophet strongly discourage.

For non-Muslim colleagues and friends, the safe reading is this: when a Muslim says "I'll send it Monday, inshallah," they usually mean they plan to send it on Monday and recognize that circumstances are in God's hands. If you need a firmer answer, it is perfectly polite to ask.

## How do you reply to inshallah?

There is no fixed reply in the hadith, so responses come from common practice. The most natural options are:

1. **Say "inshallah" back.** This is the most common response and simply agrees: "God willing, yes."
2. **Say "Ameen."** If the inshallah expresses a hope, such as "inshallah you'll recover soon," "Ameen" means "may God accept it."
3. **Reply in plain English.** "God willing" or "hopefully" is fine, especially if you are not Muslim. Nobody expects a non-Muslim to use Arabic phrases.

## Common mistakes with inshallah

The most important mistake is one many Muslims make without realizing it: adding inshallah to a dua. The Prophet said, "None of you should say: 'O Allah, forgive me if You wish; O Allah, be merciful to me if You wish,' but he should always appeal to Allah with determination" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6339). When asking God for something, you ask with confidence, not with conditions.

Other common slips include:

- **Using it to dodge.** As explained above, inshallah should not replace an honest "no."
- **Using it in place of effort.** Saying inshallah is not a substitute for preparing, studying or working. The Quran pairs trust in God with action.
- **Confusing it with mashallah.** Inshallah looks to the future; mashallah praises something that already exists. The table below shows how the two fit with the other everyday phrases.

## Inshallah and related Islamic phrases

Inshallah belongs to a family of short phrases that Muslims weave into everyday speech. Each one has a different job.

| Phrase | Meaning | When it is said |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inshallah | If God wills | About future plans and hopes |
| [Mashallah](/learn/mashallah-meaning) | What God has willed | When admiring something that already exists |
| [Bismillah](/learn/bismillah-meaning) | In the name of God | Before starting an action |
| [Alhamdulillah](/learn/alhamdulillah-meaning) | All praise is for God | After good news, or in any situation |
| [SubhanAllah](/learn/subhanallah-meaning) | Glory be to God | In amazement, or as remembrance |
| [Astaghfirullah](/learn/astaghfirullah-meaning) | I seek God's forgiveness | After a mistake, or as remembrance |

Together they form much of what Muslims call **dhikr**, the remembrance of God, explained in our guide to [what dhikr is](/learn/what-is-dhikr).

## Frequently asked questions

### What does inshallah mean in English?

Inshallah means "if God wills" or "God willing." It comes from three Arabic words: *in* (if), *sha'a* (has willed) and *Allah* (God). Muslims say it when talking about the future, following Quran 18:23-24, which tells believers not to say they will do something tomorrow without adding "if Allah wills."

### How do you write inshallah in Arabic?

Inshallah is written in Arabic as three words: إن شاء الله. Writing it as one joined word, إنشاء الله, changes the meaning, because *insha'* means "creation." In English, spellings such as inshallah, in sha Allah and insha'Allah are all used and understood.

### Is it inshallah or in sha Allah?

Both are correct transliterations of the same Arabic phrase. "Inshallah" is the most common everyday spelling, while "in sha Allah" separates the three Arabic words and is preferred by many Muslim writers because it mirrors the Arabic more closely. The meaning, "if God wills," is identical.

### How should I reply when someone says inshallah?

You can reply "inshallah" to agree, or "Ameen" when the phrase expresses a hope, such as recovery from illness. There is no fixed reply in the hadith, so a plain "God willing" or "hopefully" is fine too, especially for non-Muslims, who are not expected to use Arabic phrases.

### Can non-Muslims say inshallah?

Yes. Inshallah is an Arabic expression, and Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews use it naturally because "Allah" is simply the Arabic word for God. Non-Muslims who say it sincerely as "God willing" are using it correctly, and most Muslims appreciate the gesture.

### Should Muslims say inshallah in a dua?

No. The Prophet taught that a person should not say "O Allah, forgive me if You wish" but should ask with determination, as recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari 6339. Inshallah belongs to statements about future plans, while a dua is a confident request made directly to God.

The Kaaba is a cube-shaped stone building in the center of the Sacred Mosque in Makkah (Mecca), Saudi Arabia, and the holiest site in Islam. Muslims believe the Prophet Abraham and his son Ishmael raised its foundations as a house for the worship of one God. Muslims everywhere face it in prayer and walk around it during pilgrimage.

The Kaaba is not an object of worship. Muslims worship God alone, and the Kaaba gives them a shared direction, a meeting point and a link to Abraham. This guide covers its history, the Black Stone, why Muslims face it and circle it, and how Hajj and Umrah work.

## Key takeaways

- The Quran says Abraham and Ishmael raised the foundations of the House and prayed for God to accept their work ([Quran 2:127](/surah/al-baqarah/127)).
- The Quran calls it "the first House [of worship] established for mankind," located at Makkah ([Quran 3:96](/surah/ali-imran/96)).
- Muslims first prayed toward Jerusalem; the direction of prayer changed to the Kaaba about 16 or 17 months after the Prophet moved to Medina (Sahih al-Bukhari 40).
- Umar ibn al-Khattab said of the Black Stone, "I know that you are a stone and can neither benefit anyone nor harm anyone," and kissed it only because the Prophet had (Sahih al-Bukhari 1597).
- Hajj, the pilgrimage to the Kaaba, is required once in a lifetime of every Muslim who is able ([Quran 3:97](/surah/ali-imran/97)); 1,707,301 people performed Hajj in 2026, according to Saudi Arabia's General Authority for Statistics.

## What is the Kaaba?

**Kaaba** (*Ka'bah*, كعبة) means "cube" in Arabic, a description of its shape. It stands in the courtyard of the Sacred Mosque, *al-Masjid al-Haram*. The Quran calls it "the House" (*al-Bayt*), "the Sacred House" and "the Ancient House."

The building is made of stone and covered by a black cloth called the **kiswah**, embroidered with verses of the Quran in gold and silver thread and replaced each year. Inside, it is an empty room. There are no statues, images or objects of worship.

The Kaaba is the center point toward which roughly two billion Muslims turn five times a day. Our article on [why Muslims pray five times a day](/learn/why-muslims-pray-five-times-a-day) explains the daily rhythm of that prayer.

## Who built the Kaaba? Abraham and Ishmael

The Quran connects the Kaaba to Abraham (*Ibrahim*), the prophet honored by Jews, Christians and Muslims. Muslim tradition holds that Abraham left his wife Hagar and infant son Ishmael (*Isma'il*) in the valley of Makkah, and that years later father and son returned to build the House together.

> And [mention] when Abraham was raising the foundations of the House and [with him] Ishmael, [saying], "Our Lord, accept [this] from us. Indeed You are the Hearing, the Knowing.
>
> — [Quran 2:127](/surah/al-baqarah/127)

God told them to keep it pure for worshippers: "Purify My House for those who perform Tawaf and those who are staying [there] for worship and those who bow and prostrate [in prayer]" ([Quran 2:125](/surah/al-baqarah/125)). Abraham then prayed for Makkah to be "a secure city."

Muslims see the Kaaba as a monument to Abraham's pure belief in one God, the idea Muslims call *tawheed*. Over the centuries, however, the Arabs placed idols around it. When the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) entered Makkah in 630 CE, there were 360 idols around the Kaaba. He struck them down, reciting, "Truth has come and Falsehood will neither start nor will it reappear" (Sahih al-Bukhari 4287), words that echo a verse of the Quran. For Muslims, this restored the House to its original purpose. Read more about Abraham and the other prophets in [prophets in the Quran](/learn/prophets-in-the-quran).

## The Year of the Elephant

The most dramatic event in the Kaaba's pre-Islamic history is the attack of Abraha, a Christian Abyssinian ruler of Yemen. According to Muslim historians, he marched on Makkah with an army that included at least one war elephant, intending to destroy the Kaaba. The army was destroyed before it could reach the House.

The Quran tells the story in Surah al-Fil, "The Elephant":

> Have you not considered, [O Muhammad], how your Lord dealt with the companions of the elephant? Did He not make their plan into misguidance? And He sent against them birds in flocks, Striking them with stones of hard clay, And He made them like eaten straw.
>
> — [Quran 105:1–5](/surah/al-fil/1)

The Arabs remembered this as the Year of the Elephant, traditionally dated around 570 CE. Muslim tradition holds that the Prophet Muhammad was born that same year. ## What is the Black Stone?

The **Black Stone** (*al-Hajar al-Aswad*) is set into the eastern corner of the Kaaba, framed in silver. Muslims believe it dates back to the time of Abraham. Pilgrims begin and end each circuit around the Kaaba at its corner, and many try to kiss or touch it, or simply point toward it in a crowd.

Muslims do not worship the stone. The clearest statement comes from the second caliph, Umar ibn al-Khattab, who said as he kissed it:

"No doubt, I know that you are a stone and can neither benefit anyone nor harm anyone. Had I not seen Allah's Messenger kissing you I would not have kissed you." (Sahih al-Bukhari 1597; a similar report is in Sahih Muslim 1270.)

Umar's words sum up the Muslim view. The act has meaning only because the Prophet did it, as an act of following, not because the stone has any power.

## Do Muslims worship a cube?

No. This is a common misconception. Muslims worship only God, and Islam forbids worshipping any object, person or place. Several facts make this clear:

- **The direction once was different.** For the first period in Medina, Muslims prayed toward Jerusalem. The Kaaba became the direction of prayer by God's command, not because of anything in the building.
- **God is not in the Kaaba.** The Quran says, "And to Allah belongs the east and the west. So wherever you [might] turn, there is the Face of Allah" ([Quran 2:115](/surah/al-baqarah/115)).
- **Prayer on top of it is valid.** Classical jurists discussed praying on the roof of the Kaaba, which would make no sense if the building itself were the object of worship.
- **The stone has no power.** Umar's statement above was said in public precisely to make the point.

Instead, the Kaaba works like a shared compass. It unites Muslims across the world in one direction and one act of worship. Read more about the central idea of God's oneness in [what is tawheed](/learn/what-is-tawheed).

## What is the qibla?

The **qibla** (*qiblah*, قبلة) is the direction Muslims face in prayer: toward the Kaaba. In Medina, the Prophet prayed toward Jerusalem "for sixteen or seventeen months," hoping the direction would change to the Kaaba (Sahih al-Bukhari 40). The Quran then revealed:

> We have certainly seen the turning of your face, [O Muhammad], toward the heaven, and We will surely turn you to a qiblah with which you will be pleased. So turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Haram. And wherever you [believers] are, turn your faces toward it [in prayer]. Indeed, those who have been given the Scripture well know that it is the truth from their Lord. And Allah is not unaware of what they do.
>
> — [Quran 2:144](/surah/al-baqarah/144)

Mosques have a niche (*mihrab*) marking the qibla, and Muslims elsewhere use a compass or app. Seen from above, Muslims praying around the world form great circles converging on Makkah.

## Why do Muslims circle the Kaaba?

Walking around the Kaaba seven times counterclockwise is called **tawaf** (*tawaf*, طواف). It is part of both Hajj and Umrah, and pilgrims can also do it voluntarily at any time.

Muslims give several reasons:

- **Obedience.** God told Abraham to purify the House "for those who perform Tawaf" ([Quran 2:125](/surah/al-baqarah/125)), and the Prophet performed it.
- **God at the center.** Many scholars compare tawaf to the way everything in creation moves in orbits, with worship of God at the center of a believer's life.
- **Unity and equality.** Pilgrims of every race and class walk shoulder to shoulder, many in identical simple white garments.

## Why do Muslims go to Mecca? Hajj explained

Muslims go to Makkah to perform **Hajj** (*hajj*, حج), the pilgrimage, which is one of the [five pillars of Islam](/learn/five-pillars-of-islam). It is a duty once in a lifetime for every adult Muslim who is physically and financially able:

> In it are clear signs [such as] the standing place of Abraham. And whoever enters it shall be safe. And [due] to Allah from the people is a pilgrimage to the House - for whoever is able to find thereto a way. But whoever disbelieves - then indeed, Allah is free from need of the worlds.
>
> — [Quran 3:97](/surah/ali-imran/97)

Hajj takes place each year in the Islamic month of Dhu al-Hijjah. In 2026, Saudi Arabia's General Authority for Statistics counted 1,707,301 pilgrims. Only Muslims may enter Makkah.

### The main rites of Hajj

1. **Ihram.** Pilgrims enter a state of consecration, and men wear two plain white cloths, removing outward signs of wealth.
2. **Tawaf.** They circle the Kaaba seven times.
3. **Sa'i.** They walk seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwah, remembering Hagar's search for water for Ishmael ([Quran 2:158](/surah/al-baqarah/158)).
4. **Mina.** They spend the 8th of Dhu al-Hijjah in a tent city outside Makkah.
5. **Arafat.** On the 9th, the most important day, they stand in prayer on the plain of Arafat from midday to sunset.
6. **Muzdalifah.** They spend the night under the open sky and collect pebbles.
7. **Stoning the pillars.** At Mina they throw pebbles at three pillars, recalling, according to early reports, Abraham's rejection of Satan's temptation.
8. **Sacrifice.** An animal is sacrificed, and the meat is shared with the poor. Muslims worldwide do the same on Eid al-Adha.
9. **Shaving or trimming the hair** and a final tawaf complete the pilgrimage.

The Prophet's own Hajj in 632 CE, his only one, set the pattern still followed today. It is described in detail by his companion Jabir ibn Abdullah in Sahih Muslim 1218.

## Umrah vs Hajj: what is the difference?

**Umrah** is often called the "lesser pilgrimage." It shares some rites with Hajj but is shorter and can be done at any time.

| Feature | Hajj | Umrah |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Obligation | Required once in a lifetime for those able | Recommended; some scholars consider it obligatory once |
| Timing | Only on fixed days, 8th to 13th of Dhu al-Hijjah | Any time of year |
| Duration | About five to six days | A few hours |
| Main rites | Ihram, tawaf, sa'i, Mina, Arafat, Muzdalifah, stoning, sacrifice | Ihram, tawaf, sa'i, shaving or trimming |
| Day of Arafat | Essential | Not included |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the Kaaba?

The Kaaba is a cube-shaped stone building in the Sacred Mosque in Makkah, Saudi Arabia, and Islam's holiest site. Muslims believe Abraham and Ishmael built it to worship one God. Muslims face it in their five daily prayers and walk around it during Hajj and Umrah, but they worship God alone, not the building.

### Why do Muslims worship a cube?

They do not. Muslims worship only God and face the Kaaba as a shared direction of prayer that God commanded in Quran 2:144. Muslims prayed toward Jerusalem before the qibla changed, and the caliph Umar said of the Black Stone that it can neither benefit nor harm anyone (Sahih al-Bukhari 1597).

### Why do Muslims circle the Kaaba?

Circling the Kaaba seven times, called tawaf, is an act of worship commanded in Quran 2:125 and practiced by the Prophet. Muslims see it as placing God at the center of life, and it expresses unity as pilgrims of every background walk together. It is a core rite of both Hajj and Umrah.

### Why do Muslims go to Mecca?

Muslims go to Makkah to perform Hajj, the pilgrimage that is one of the five pillars of Islam and a duty once in a lifetime for those able, as stated in Quran 3:97. They also go for Umrah, a shorter pilgrimage that can be done at any time. In 2026, 1,707,301 people performed Hajj.

### What is the Black Stone?

The Black Stone is a stone set into the eastern corner of the Kaaba, which Muslims believe dates to the time of Abraham. Pilgrims begin each circuit of tawaf there and may kiss or touch it, following the Prophet's example. Muslims do not believe it has any power, as Umar ibn al-Khattab made clear.

### What is the difference between Hajj and Umrah?

Hajj is obligatory once in a lifetime for those able and is performed only on set days in Dhu al-Hijjah, including the standing at Arafat. Umrah is a shorter pilgrimage of ihram, tawaf, sa'i and trimming the hair. It can be done at any time of year and is not one of the five pillars.

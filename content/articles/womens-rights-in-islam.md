The Quran grants women equal spiritual worth with men and specific legal rights: to own and manage property, to keep their earnings, to inherit, to consent to marriage, to receive a marriage gift (mahr) and to seek divorce. Many of these rights were unusual in the 7th century. Muslim societies have often failed to honor them fully.

Discussions of women in Islam tend to swing between two extremes: "Islam oppresses women" and "Islam perfectly liberated women." Neither is the full picture. This guide sets out what the Quran and authentic hadith grant women, explains the verses that are most debated, and looks honestly at where Muslim cultures fall short of their own texts.

## Key takeaways

- The Quran names women and men side by side for every quality of faith and promises both the same reward (Quran 33:35).
- Women have a fixed, obligatory share of inheritance (Quran 4:7), though in several cases a daughter receives half a son's share (Quran 4:11) because men carry the duty of financial support.
- A previously married woman may not be married without her consultation, and a virgin not without her permission (Sahih al-Bukhari 5136); the Prophet annulled a marriage made against a woman's will (Sahih al-Bukhari 5138).
- Women may end a marriage through *khul'*, as the wife of Thabit ibn Qays did with the Prophet's approval (Sahih al-Bukhari 5273).
- Pew's 2013 survey found wide gaps between these principles and popular attitudes; for example, support for a woman's right to divorce ranged from 94% in Bosnia-Herzegovina to 8% in Malaysia.

## Are men and women spiritually equal in Islam?

Yes. In Islamic belief, women and men are created from a single soul ([Quran 4:1](/surah/an-nisa/1)), are equally responsible for faith and worship, and are judged by the same standard. The clearest statement is this verse:

> Indeed, the Muslim men and Muslim women, the believing men and believing women, the obedient men and obedient women, the truthful men and truthful women, the patient men and patient women, the humble men and humble women, the charitable men and charitable women, the fasting men and fasting women, the men who guard their private parts and the women who do so, and the men who remember Allah often and the women who do so - for them Allah has prepared forgiveness and a great reward.
>
> — [Quran 33:35](/surah/al-ahzab/35)

The Quran also calls believing men and women "allies of one another" who together "enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong" ([Quran 9:71](/surah/at-tawbah/71)). Eve is not blamed for the fall: the Quran describes Adam and his wife as both tempted and both forgiven. You can follow that story on the [journey of Adam](/timeline/journey/adam).

## What rights did the Quran give women in the 7th century?

To understand why many Muslims see the Quran as reforming, it helps to know what it changed. In pre-Islamic Arabia, women in many tribes could not inherit, could themselves be "inherited" by a dead husband's relatives, and could be married off without a say. The Quran addressed each of these.

| Right | What the Quran or Sunnah says | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inheritance | Women receive an obligatory share, "be it little or much" | Quran 4:7 |
| Not being inherited | "It is not lawful for you to inherit women by compulsion" | Quran 4:19 |
| Property and earnings | "For women is a share of what they have earned" | Quran 4:32 |
| Marriage gift (mahr) | The bride receives a gift that belongs to her alone | Quran 4:4 |
| Consent to marriage | A woman must be consulted or give permission | Sahih al-Bukhari 5136 |
| Financial support | The husband must provide for his wife, even if she is wealthy | Quran 4:34, 65:6 |
| Divorce | A wife may seek separation through khul' | Quran 2:229; Sahih al-Bukhari 5273 |
| Kind treatment | "Live with them in kindness" | Quran 4:19 |

### Property and work

Under Islamic law, a married woman keeps her own name, property and income. Her husband has no right to her money, while she has a right to his support. Can Muslim women work? Yes: nothing in the Quran forbids it, and early Muslim women traded, farmed, nursed the wounded and ran businesses. Scholars discuss conditions, such as safety and family responsibilities, but a woman's earnings are her own.

### Consent and the mahr

The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) said that a previously married woman should not be married without being consulted, and a virgin not without her permission (Sahih al-Bukhari 5136). When Khansa bint Khidam's father married her against her will, the Prophet declared the marriage invalid (Sahih al-Bukhari 5138). The **mahr** (*mahr*, مهر), a gift from the groom, belongs to the bride, and the Quran tells men to give it "graciously" ([Quran 4:4](/surah/an-nisa/4)).

### Divorce rights for women

A man may divorce through *talaq*, but the Quran limits it and requires that a wife be released "with good treatment" ([Quran 2:229](/surah/al-baqarah/229)). A woman can end the marriage through **khul'**, usually by returning her mahr. In Sahih al-Bukhari 5273, the wife of Thabit ibn Qays told the Prophet she had no complaint about her husband's character or religion but could not stay with him; the Prophet asked her to return the garden he had given her and told Thabit to divorce her. She could also seek judicial divorce for harm, desertion or lack of support, though the grounds differ between schools.

## How does inheritance work for women in Islam?

The inheritance verses are the most often cited example of inequality, so they deserve a careful look:

> Allah instructs you concerning your children: for the male, what is equal to the share of two females. But if there are [only] daughters, two or more, for them is two thirds of one's estate. And if there is only one, for her is half. And for one's parents, to each one of them is a sixth of his estate if he left children. But if he had no children and the parents [alone] inherit from him, then for his mother is one third. And if he had brothers [or sisters], for his mother is a sixth, after any bequest he [may have] made or debt. Your parents or your children - you know not which of them are nearest to you in benefit. [These shares are] an obligation [imposed] by Allah. Indeed, Allah is ever Knowing and Wise.
>
> — [Quran 4:11](/surah/an-nisa/11)

Three points help explain it:

1. **The 2:1 ratio applies in some cases, not all.** In the same verse, a mother and father each receive a sixth when the deceased has children. Siblings from the mother's side share equally ([Quran 4:12](/surah/an-nisa/12)), and in some situations a woman inherits more than a man would.
2. **Shares are linked to obligations.** A man must pay the mahr and support his wife, children and sometimes other relatives. A woman's share is hers alone, with no duty to spend it on the household.
3. **It was a large change.** In the 7th century, guaranteeing women any fixed share was a reform.

Critics point out that today many women support households, so the rationale may not match modern life. Some Muslim reformers have argued for equal shares; Tunisia's government proposed equal inheritance in 2018, which divided opinion. Most scholars hold that the Quranic shares are fixed and not open to change.

## Which verses about women are most debated?

Some verses are difficult for modern readers, and Muslims themselves discuss them.

- **Quran 4:34** says men are "in charge of" women and, in the case of a wife's serious misconduct, lists advice, separation in bed and finally "strike them." Classical scholars restricted this heavily, some calling for it to be symbolic and harmless, and the Prophet himself never struck a woman. Many modern scholars argue it should not be acted on at all, and some translators read the Arabic verb differently. The verse remains a real point of debate.
- **Quran 2:282** asks for two women in place of one man as witnesses in a commercial debt contract. Many scholars limit this to financial contracts of that era; others apply it more broadly.
- **Quran 2:228** says men have "a degree" over women, which commentators link to financial responsibility, while the same verse states women have rights "similar to" those expected of them.

The Prophet's own example is often cited against harsh readings: "The best of you is the best to his wives, and I am the best of you to my wives" (Jami at-Tirmidhi 3895).

## Khadijah, Aisha and women scholars

The earliest history of Islam gives women central roles.

- **Khadijah** was a successful merchant in Makkah who employed Muhammad to lead her trade caravan, then proposed marriage to him. She was the first person to accept Islam and supported the Prophet through the hardest years.
- **Aisha** became one of the most important teachers of the early Muslim community. Some 2,210 hadith are narrated through her, on law, ritual, inheritance and the Prophet's character, and senior companions came to her with legal questions.
- **Women scholars** continued that tradition. The Oxford scholar Mohammad Akram Nadwi compiled a biographical dictionary of women hadith scholars that runs to some forty volumes. Fatima al-Fihri founded the mosque that grew into al-Qarawiyyin in Fez, Morocco, in the 9th century, which UNESCO and Guinness describe as the oldest continually operating institution of higher learning.

The Quran also honors Mary as chosen "above the women of the worlds," as our guide to [Mary in the Quran](/learn/mary-in-the-quran) explains.

## Where do Muslim cultures fall short?

A fair account has to say plainly that the rights above are often not respected in practice.

- **Inheritance:** In parts of South Asia, the Middle East and Africa, women are pressured to give up their Quranic share to brothers.
- **Forced and child marriage:** Despite the requirement of consent, forced marriages happen in some communities, and child marriage remains legal or common in several Muslim-majority countries.
- **Divorce:** In many places, women face long, costly court processes to obtain divorce, while men can divorce easily.
- **Education and work:** Afghanistan's Taliban government has barred girls from secondary school and women from university since 2021–2022, a policy condemned by scholars and governments across the Muslim world.
- **Honor-based violence and domestic abuse** occur in some communities, and some perpetrators wrongly claim religious justification.

Popular attitudes vary widely. In Pew Research Center's [2013 survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2013/04/30/the-worlds-muslims-religion-politics-society-women-in-society/), majorities in 20 of 23 countries said a wife must always obey her husband. Support for a woman's right to initiate divorce ranged from 94% in Bosnia-Herzegovina and 81% in Tunisia to 22% in Egypt and 8% in Malaysia.

Many Muslim women activists and scholars argue that the solution is not to abandon Islam but to recover the rights its sources already grant. Others argue that some classical rulings need rethinking. Both positions are active in Muslim debate today.

## Frequently asked questions

### What rights do women have in Islam?

According to the Quran and Sunnah, women have equal spiritual worth with men and rights to own property, keep their earnings, inherit, consent to marriage, receive a mahr, be financially supported by their husbands and seek divorce. How well these rights are protected depends heavily on the laws and culture of each country and family.

### How does inheritance work for women in Islam?

The Quran gives women a fixed, obligatory share of inheritance (4:7). In several cases, such as between a son and a daughter, the daughter receives half the son's share (4:11), which scholars link to men's duty to support the family. In other cases, such as parents of a deceased person with children, men and women receive equal shares.

### Can Muslim women work?

Yes. Nothing in the Quran forbids women from working, and early Muslim women traded and ran businesses, including the Prophet's wife Khadijah. A woman's income belongs to her, and she has no religious duty to spend it on the household. Scholars discuss conditions such as safety and family responsibilities, but the principle of women working is widely accepted.

### Can a Muslim woman divorce her husband?

Yes. A woman can end her marriage through khul', usually by returning her mahr, as the wife of Thabit ibn Qays did with the Prophet's approval (Sahih al-Bukhari 5273). She may also seek a judicial divorce for harm, desertion or lack of support. In practice, some countries make this process slow and difficult, which women's rights advocates criticize.

### Does Islam allow forced marriage?

No. The Prophet taught that a woman must be consulted or give her permission before marriage (Sahih al-Bukhari 5136), and he annulled a marriage made against a woman's will (Sahih al-Bukhari 5138). Forced marriages that still occur in some communities violate this teaching, and scholars across the schools of law agree that a woman's consent is required.

### Were there female scholars in Islam?

Yes, thousands. Aisha was among the most important early teachers of hadith and law. Mohammad Akram Nadwi's biographical dictionary of women hadith scholars runs to about forty volumes. Fatima al-Fihri founded the institution in Fez that became the University of al-Qarawiyyin, and women scholars taught men and women across the Muslim world for centuries.
